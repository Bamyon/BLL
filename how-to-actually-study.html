How to Actually Study (Not Just Read)

Most students don't have a studying problem. They have a re-reading problem — and the two feel identical until the exam starts.

Why re-reading tricks you

When you re-read a page, your brain recognizes the words. Recognition feels like understanding, so your brain reports back "yep, I know this" and moves on. But recognition and recall are two different skills — and exams only ever test recall. That's the gap that catches people off guard.

The fix: close the book, then explain it

After reading a section, close it. Explain the idea out loud, in your own words, as if teaching a friend who's never heard of it. If you get stuck, that's not failure — that's exactly the gap you needed to find before the exam did.

Three habits that actually work

  • Attempt before you read. Try the problem first, even badly. It primes your brain to notice the useful part of the explanation when you finally read it.
  • Space it out. Reviewing something four times over two weeks beats reviewing it four times in one night, even though it feels less efficient in the moment.
  • Teach it, don't just review it. Explaining a concept to someone — or even to an empty room — surfaces the exact spots where your understanding is thin.

Where BLL fits in

Every chapter on BLL is built around this — plain words before symbols, worked examples for everything, and nothing marked "obvious." The goal isn't to give you more to read. It's to make the first read actually stick.
